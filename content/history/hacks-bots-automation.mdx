Diablo II's closed realms had a valuable item economy. The game's long, repetitive boss runs were easy to automate. Together, these made Diablo II one of the most heavily botted games of its time. This page documents that history. It isn't a place to find cheating software for Blizzard's realms. Some tools from this history live on as open-source projects for singleplayer and for private servers that allow them. SlashDiablo, for example, provides [its own maphack](/tools/slashdiablo-maphack). Those are listed under [maphacks & automation](/tools#automation), with the same caveat.

## Client-side hacks

The most common hacks ran on the player's own machine and read the game's memory.

- **Maphacks** revealed the whole map as soon as you entered an area. They also showed monsters, missiles and items the player couldn't otherwise see.
- **Pickit** automatically picked up items that matched configured rules.
- **Chicken** tools automatically left the game or drank potions when health dropped below a threshold. The name came from "chickening out".

## Bots

Bots played the game without a player. Early bots repeated a single simple run, and they were often named after it: Pindlebot killed Pindleskin, and Mephisto bots ran Mephisto again and again. Later bots handled complex routes, whole runs through several bosses, trading and item management.

Packet bots went further. Rather than driving the game client, they spoke the game's network protocol directly. That let them run many characters at once with very little overhead.

## Duping

Item duplication, or duping, exploited server and synchronisation bugs to copy items. Duped high-value items flooded the economy repeatedly. Blizzard periodically removed items it could identify as duped, which created its own history of confusion and lost trades.

## Blizzard's response

Blizzard responded with mass account bans, server-side checks and client anti-cheat. Warden, Blizzard's anti-cheat system, scanned for known cheating tools. It's generally associated with the mid-2000s. Detection and evasion went back and forth for years.
